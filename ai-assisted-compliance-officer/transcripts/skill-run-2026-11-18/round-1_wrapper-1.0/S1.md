## Answer

A **policy** says what the organization requires and why. A **procedure** says how a particular person or role carries out that requirement, step by step.

**Policy**
- States the rule, its purpose and its scope: who and what it covers.
- Sets the principles, the required and prohibited conduct, and who is accountable.
- Is approved at a senior level (Board or executive management) and changes rarely.
- Example: "All third parties must be screened against applicable sanctions lists before onboarding. Compliance owns this requirement."

**Procedure**
- Turns the policy into steps someone can carry out: who acts, in what order, with which tool or form, by when and what record they keep.
- Covers exceptions, escalation and the evidence each step leaves behind.
- Is owned at the operational level and is updated whenever systems or workflows change.
- Example: "Procurement enters the vendor in the screening tool; any potential match goes to Compliance within one business day; the analyst records the outcome in the vendor file before a purchase order can be issued."

**Why the distinction matters in practice**
- Auditors and regulators test both. They check that the policy exists, then that the procedure is actually followed and leaves evidence.
- A policy with no procedure behind it is a statement of intent. A procedure with no policy above it has no clear authority or purpose.
- Keeping them separate lets operations revise the steps without sending the policy back to the Board each time.
- Issuing a policy does not mean it has been adopted. Adoption shows in procedures that are followed and in the records they produce.

Two related layers often sit alongside them: **standards**, which are specific mandatory requirements such as a minimum password length, and **guidelines**, which are recommended but not mandatory practice.
