## Treat this as two separate issues

1. **The safety allegation.** This must be assessed on its merits regardless of the demand. If there is a real hazard, the company's exposure comes from the hazard, not the email.
2. **The payment demand.** How you respond creates its own legal risk, including retaliation, unlawful restriction of whistleblowing and statements that could later be used against the company.

The biggest mistake would be treating this only as a shakedown and ignoring the safety substance, or only as a money problem.

## Immediate actions (safe regardless of facts)

1. **The CEO should not reply.** No acknowledgment, no counteroffer, no "let's talk." Route all communication through Legal.
2. **Preservation action should be considered now.**
   - Preserve the original email in native form with full headers. Don't just forward it.
   - Identify likely custodians: CEO, the employee's former manager, plant management, EHS/safety, HR, and whoever handled the separation.
   - Suspend routine deletion for relevant safety records, inspection logs, incident reports, maintenance records, internal safety complaints, and the employee's personnel and separation file.
   - Keep a record of who received any hold, when, how, and their acknowledgment.
3. **Restrict circulation to need-to-know.** Keep internal discussion under Legal's direction to support privilege. Avoid casual written characterizations like "extortion," "disgruntled," or "nothing to this."
4. **Assess immediate safety risk.** If the allegations point to a hazard that could hurt someone now, quietly route them to your EHS lead under Legal's direction today, independent of the demand.
5. **Engage outside employment counsel now**, ideally with whistleblower and safety-regulatory experience. **Escalate to specialist or outside counsel.**
6. **Consider insurance notice promptly.** Check EPLI and D&O policies. A written demand may count as a "claim" under the policy, and late notice can jeopardize coverage.

## What not to do

- **Do not retaliate**, including against a former employee. Negative references, withholding owed benefits or pay, or aggressive legal threats can create a retaliation claim even after employment ends.
- **Do not offer or agree to any payment conditioned on the person not reporting to regulators.** Many jurisdictions prohibit agreements that restrict reporting to government agencies, such as safety regulators, or the SEC for public companies. Any settlement would need carefully drafted carve-outs. **Requires current legal verification. Jurisdiction-specific review required.**
- **Do not contact law enforcement or label this criminal extortion** without counsel's advice. Whether this is unlawful extortion or a protected complaint combined with a settlement demand depends heavily on the facts and jurisdiction.
- **Do not alter, "clean up," or remediate safety records.** Fix real hazards, but document the fix. Never revise the historical record.
