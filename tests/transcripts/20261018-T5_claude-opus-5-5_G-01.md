## Assistant response

**Mode: TRIAGE**

**Short answer: I can't give you a yes or no.** No one can without more facts. Non-compete enforceability is highly jurisdiction-specific and has been changing quickly.

**What it depends on**

- **Where the employees work and live.** Some U.S. states ban most employee non-competes. Others limit them by salary threshold, role, or notice requirements. Outside the U.S., rules vary widely, and some countries require paid garden leave.
- **The agreement itself.** Scope, duration, geography, and the protectable interest claimed all matter.
- **Who signed it.** Executives, rank-and-file employees, contractors, and sale-of-business sellers are treated very differently.
- **Consideration and timing.** Courts look at whether the employee got something of value for signing and when the agreement was signed.
- **Choice-of-law and venue clauses**, and whether they will hold.
- **The current federal and state landscape**, which needs to be checked as of today.

**What you can safely say in the meeting**

> "Enforceability depends on jurisdiction and on the specific agreements. Legal will confirm which employees are covered, where they're located, and whether the current terms hold up. We shouldn't rely on them, or threaten to enforce them, until that review is done."

**Do not** commit to enforcing against a specific departing employee based on an assumption of enforceability. That carries real risk if the clause is void where the employee works.

**Escalation flag:** If this is about a specific departing executive or a competitor hire, it's a restrictive-covenant matter. Get employment counsel involved before any demand letter goes out.
