**Mode:** TRIAGE -> ESCALATION (may move to INVESTIGATION SUPPORT and LITIGATION RESPONSE)

This is high-risk. It combines a safety allegation, a former-employee demand, and potential regulatory, retaliation and litigation exposure. This is an initial orientation, not a legal conclusion. **Escalate to specialist or outside counsel.**

## Do these now, before we go further

1. **Don't respond, and have the CEO not respond.** No reply, call, or "just checking in" from anyone, and no forwarding or discussing it informally. Casual replies can create admissions, undercut privilege, or look retaliatory.
2. **Escalate today** to the General Counsel or senior legal lead. Employment and EHS/safety leadership should be brought in on a need-to-know basis, and the CEO should route all communications through legal.
3. **Preservation action should be considered now.** Litigation or a regulatory inquiry is reasonably anticipated. Preserve the email itself with its metadata, plus the former employee's personnel file, separation documents, and email and messaging data. Preserve plant safety records (incident logs, inspections, near-miss reports, maintenance, training, prior complaints, regulator correspondence) and the CEO's and relevant managers' communications. Suspend routine deletion for those categories and document who received the hold and when.
4. **Treat the safety allegation as a separate track from the money demand.** Whatever happens with the severance request, the safety claim needs a credible, privileged assessment. If there is an actual hazard, the plant may need to act whatever the former employee does.
5. **Don't characterize the email as "extortion" or "blackmail" internally or externally.** Whether it is legally improper depends on jurisdiction and facts. Reporting safety concerns may be protected activity, and mislabeling it can hurt you later. **Jurisdiction-specific review required.**
6. **Consider insurance notice** (EPLI, D&O, general liability) promptly. Late notice can jeopardize coverage.
7. **No adverse action** against the former employee or anyone connected to them, and no contact with them by managers, until counsel advises.

## Why this is an escalation matter

- **Safety allegation:** possible regulatory reporting duties, agency exposure, and real-world harm if true.
- **Whistleblower/retaliation risk:** reporting to regulators is often legally protected, and agreements generally can't bar it. This is jurisdiction-specific and **requires current legal verification**.
- **Payment for silence:** paying more severance in exchange for not disclosing safety issues carries risk. It could be characterized badly and may be unenforceable or problematic as to regulator reporting. Don't commit to any payment, or signal willingness to pay, before counsel reviews.
- **Possible disclosure issues** if the company is public or has lenders, customers or insurers with notice or representation obligations. **Unknown/Insufficient data.**

## Questions I need answered

2. **Separation terms:** Did the employee sign a separation agreement or release? When did they leave, and why (resignation, termination, layoff)? Do their confidentiality terms carve out agency reporting?
3. **Email content:** Can you share the exact wording, with identifying details removed if needed? Does it describe specific hazards, or is it vague?
4. **Prior history:** Did the employee raise safety concerns while employed? Are there any prior complaints, incidents, citations or open regulator matters?
5. **Who knows:** Who has seen the email, and has anyone replied or discussed it?
6. **Support:** Is outside counsel already engaged, and who is the decision owner?
7. **Deliverable:** What do you want next: an escalation memo, a hold notice, a response strategy, or an investigation plan for the safety claims?
