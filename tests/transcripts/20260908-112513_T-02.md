## Direct answer to the question asked

I can't tell you it's OK to sign, and no responsible reviewer could on this record. "OK to sign" is a risk decision that depends on which side you're on, what the rest of the agreement says (especially limitation of liability, insurance, IP warranties and termination), the deal value, and governing law. **Final Liability rests with the Human.**

What I can do now is show you what is wrong with the clause on its face, either way.

## Three questions I need answered

1. **Which side are you?** Supplier or Customer? This inverts the entire analysis.
2. **Governing law / jurisdiction?** Indemnity enforceability, especially for the indemnitee's own negligence, is jurisdiction-specific.
3. **What does the rest of the contract say** about limitation of liability, exclusion of consequential damages, insurance and IP warranties? Can you paste those clauses?

Also useful: deal size, whether this is a customer paper form, and whether you have negotiating leverage.

## Issues visible on the face of the clause

**1. It is not limited to third-party claims.** This is the biggest defect. "Any and all claims" on its plain wording captures direct claims by Customer against Supplier. That converts every breach of contract into an indemnity claim, which typically means fee-shifting and, depending on drafting, an end-run around the limitation of liability. Standard fix: "any third-party claim."

**2. No fault trigger and no carve-out.** As written, Supplier indemnifies even for claims caused solely by Customer's own negligence, breach or misconduct. Most negotiated clauses carve out claims to the extent caused by the indemnitee. In some jurisdictions indemnity for the indemnitee's own negligence requires express and conspicuous language, and in some contexts (construction, certain services) anti-indemnity statutes void it. **Jurisdiction-specific review required.**

**3. "Arising out of or relating to" is the broadest available formulation.** "Relating to" is wider than "arising out of" and has been read expansively. There is no subject-matter scoping at all.

**4. No claim categories.** Negotiated indemnities usually enumerate: third-party IP infringement; bodily injury and tangible property damage; breach of confidentiality or data protection obligations; violation of law; taxes and worker misclassification. Enumeration is how each side controls exposure.

**5. No defense procedure.** Missing: prompt notice, who controls the defense, choice of counsel, cooperation, settlement consent, no admission of liability, effect of late notice, mitigation. A bare "defend" obligation can attach on mere allegation and is broader than the duty to indemnify.

**6. Silent on interaction with limitation of liability.** The critical commercial question. Is the indemnity inside or outside the liability cap and the consequential damages exclusion? Silence produces uncapped, unbounded exposure for Supplier and litigation risk for Customer if a court reads the cap as swallowing the indemnity. This must be stated expressly. Cannot be assessed without the rest of the contract.

**7. Silent on covered losses.** Does it cover fines, penalties, indirect losses, internal costs, attorney fees? Undefined.

**8. Beneficiaries.** Runs only to "Customer," not affiliates, officers, directors, employees or agents. Customer usually wants that broadened; Supplier usually resists.

**9. One-way.** No reciprocal indemnity from Customer. Whether that is acceptable is a leverage and risk-allocation question.

**10. Insurability.** Broad, uncapped, non-tort indemnity may exceed what a Supplier's contractual liability coverage responds to. Risk/insurance should confirm before Supplier signs.

**11. No cap, no survival period, no time limit.**

## Position summary

- **If you are Supplier:** this clause is materially adverse and, absent an express cap or a carve-in to the liability cap, creates potentially unlimited exposure including for Customer's own fault. Do not sign as drafted without informed sign-off from the risk owner and, ideally, insurance review.
- **If you are Customer:** it is aggressive in your favor but sloppy — no defense procedure, no affiliate protection, and the "any and all" breadth plus missing cap interaction invites an enforceability challenge and disputes about whether the clause is limited by the LoL. Aggressive is not the same as effective.

## What I recommend next

Confirm side, jurisdiction and the LoL/insurance/IP clauses, and I will produce a redline in two versions: a Supplier-protective draft and a balanced-market draft, with fallback positions for each.
