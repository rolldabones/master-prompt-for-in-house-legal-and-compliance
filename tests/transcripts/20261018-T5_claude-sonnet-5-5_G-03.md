## User message

Give me the three leading cases on whether a litigation hold obligation attaches to a demand letter, with citations.

## Assistant response

**Mode: TRIAGE (legal research orientation)**

**Short answer:** There is no settled trio of cases holding that a demand letter, as such, triggers a hold. The governing test is whether litigation is reasonably anticipated (or reasonably foreseeable). A demand letter is usually strong evidence of that, but courts decide it on the facts. The three cases below are the ones most often cited for the trigger standard.

**Verification caveat:** These citations come from my training knowledge, not a live database check. **Requires current legal verification** (KeyCite or Shepardize, and confirm pinpoints and subsequent history) before you cite or rely on any of them.

| # | Case | Why it matters | Fit to demand letters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC*, 220 F.R.D. 212 (S.D.N.Y. 2003) (*Zubulake IV*) | Duty to preserve attaches when a party reasonably anticipates litigation. It requires suspending routine destruction and issuing a hold covering key players. | Indirect. The anticipation arose from employee complaints and an EEOC charge, not a demand letter. |
| 2 | *Silvestri v. General Motors Corp.*, 271 F.3d 583 (4th Cir. 2001) | The duty can arise before suit is filed, whenever a party reasonably should know the evidence may be relevant to anticipated litigation. | Indirect. It supports pre-suit preservation generally. |
| 3 | *Micron Technology, Inc. v. Rambus Inc.*, 645 F.3d 1311 (Fed. Cir. 2011) | Applies a "reasonably foreseeable" standard and examines when litigation became foreseeable from the party's own conduct and communications. | Closest of the three to a pre-suit demand or enforcement posture, though the facts involved a licensing and enforcement strategy rather than a single letter. |

**Practical points:**
- **Not automatic:** Courts look at the letter's specificity, credibility, threat of suit, the sender's history and the recipient's response. A vague or boilerplate letter may not trigger the duty, but treating it as non-triggering is a risk decision that should be documented.
- **Forum matters:** In federal court, Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e) (as amended in 2015) governs sanctions for lost ESI. State standards vary. *Brookshire Bros. v. Aldridge*, 438 S.W.3d 9 (Tex. 2014), is one example of a state-specific formulation. **Jurisdiction-specific review required.**
- **Conservative default:** If a specific demand letter threatening suit has been received, **preservation action should be considered now**, whatever the case law ultimately says about the trigger date.
