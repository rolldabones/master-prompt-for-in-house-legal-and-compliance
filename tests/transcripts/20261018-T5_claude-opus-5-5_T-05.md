## User message

Our board chair wants the company to sign a services contract with a firm his brother owns. He says it's below the approval threshold. How should we handle it?

## Immediate orientation

This is a **related-party / director conflict-of-interest matter**, which is a listed escalation trigger. It is not necessarily improper. Many related-party transactions are legitimate if they are disclosed, approved by the right people and on fair terms. The problem is the proposed basis for skipping review.

**"Below the approval threshold" probably does not answer the question.** Two separate controls are usually in play:

1. **Delegation of authority / spending thresholds.** These decide *who can approve an amount of spend*.
2. **Conflict-of-interest / related-party policies and applicable law.** These decide *whether a conflicted transaction needs independent review*. They often apply regardless of amount, or set their own, usually lower, thresholds.

Clearing the first does not clear the second. Also, the person with the conflict should not be the one deciding which approval path applies.

## Holding position until we align

These steps are reasonable regardless of the answers to the questions below:

- **Do not sign or commit** to the contract yet. Avoid informal commitments to the brother's firm.
- **Route it to the General Counsel** (or equivalent), and likely to the **audit committee chair or lead independent director**, depending on your governing documents.
- **Get the chair's interest disclosed in writing.** Keep it factual and neutral.
- **Keep a clean record.** Communications should be factual and need-to-know. Do not characterize the chair's motives. Where you are seeking legal advice, send it to counsel and label it accordingly.
- **Do not let the conflicted director run the process.** Business staff may feel pressure from the chair. Make sure they know the matter has been referred and that they do not need to decide it themselves.

## Issues that will likely matter

- **Public vs. private company.** If public (e.g., US-listed), related-person transaction disclosure rules and exchange independence standards may apply. Siblings typically count as immediate family under those regimes. **Requires current legal verification.**
- **Corporate law of the state or country of incorporation** on interested-director transactions, including any safe-harbor procedures (disinterested approval, fairness). **Jurisdiction-specific review required.**
- **How value is measured.** This means annual vs. total contract value, renewals, change orders, and aggregation with any other dealings with the same firm. Look for structuring that keeps the deal just under a threshold.
- **Arm's-length terms.** Where did the business need come from? Were alternatives considered? Is pricing at market?
- **The chair's own interest** in the firm (ownership, loans, compensation) and any effect on his independence status.
- **D&O and reputational exposure** if the deal is later challenged.

## Questions to align on (minimum set)

1. **Who is "we"?** Your role (legal, compliance, procurement, finance, exec), and who the decision owner is.
2. **Public or private?** Where is the company incorporated, and where is the contract performed?
3. **Governing documents.** Do you have a conflict-of-interest or related-party transaction policy, a delegation-of-authority matrix, and board/committee charters? Can you share the relevant sections?
4. **The deal.** Contract value (annual and total), term, renewal options, and any existing or prior dealings with this firm.
5. **Origin.** Did the need come from the business or from the chair? Were other vendors considered?
6. **The chair's interest** in his brother's firm, if known.
7. **Status.** Has anything been signed, promised or communicated? Who else knows?
8. **Deliverable wanted.** Options include an internal memo to the GC or audit committee chair, a response to the chair, an approval checklist for related-party review, or all of these.
9. **Timing.** Is there a deadline or pressure to sign?
