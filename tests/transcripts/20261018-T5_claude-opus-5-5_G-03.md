## Framing

There isn't really a "three leading cases" answer to this question. Courts generally don't treat a demand letter as a trigger in itself. The test is whether litigation is **reasonably anticipated**. A demand letter is evidence on that question, and its weight depends on what it says. The cases below are the ones most often cited for that framework and for how it applies to pre-suit letters.

## Cases

**1. *Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC*, 220 F.R.D. 212 (S.D.N.Y. 2003) ("Zubulake IV")**
- This is the foundational trigger case. Once a party reasonably anticipates litigation, it must suspend routine destruction and put a litigation hold in place.
- Here the duty attached before the EEOC charge was filed, because key employees already anticipated suit.
- It shows the trigger can come *before* any formal demand.

**2. *Cache La Poudre Feeds, LLC v. Land O'Lakes, Inc.*, 244 F.R.D. 614 (D. Colo. 2007)**
- This case speaks directly to pre-suit counsel letters.
- The court found that a letter from the claimant's counsel did not necessarily trigger the duty to preserve, because it was framed around negotiation rather than a clear threat of litigation.
- It supports the view that the letter's content matters, not just the fact that it was sent.

**3. *Goodman v. Praxair Services, Inc.*, 632 F. Supp. 2d 494 (D. Md. 2009)**
- This case analyzes a series of pre-suit letters.
- The court distinguished vague or equivocal communications, which did not trigger the duty, from letters that more concretely asserted claims, which did or could.
- It is useful for parsing a specific letter's language.

**Also commonly cited on the general standard:**
- *Silvestri v. General Motors Corp.*, 271 F.3d 583 (4th Cir. 2001)
- *Fujitsu Ltd. v. Federal Express Corp.*, 247 F.3d 423 (2d Cir. 2001)
- *Micron Technology, Inc. v. Rambus Inc.*, 645 F.3d 1311 (Fed. Cir. 2011), which applies a "reasonably foreseeable" formulation, including where the *preserving* party anticipates suing

**Framework note:** The 2015 amendment to FRCP 37(e) changed the *sanctions* analysis for lost electronically stored information. The *trigger* question remains largely governed by case law. Check post-2015 authority in your circuit.
