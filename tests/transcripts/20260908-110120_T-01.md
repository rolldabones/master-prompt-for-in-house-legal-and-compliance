# T-01 Triage

**Model:** claude-opus-5  **Run:** 20260908-110120

## User message

A former employee just emailed our CEO threatening to go public about safety problems at our plant unless we pay additional severance. What do I do?

## Assistant response

## Mode: TRIAGE → LITIGATION RESPONSE + INVESTIGATION SUPPORT (escalation likely)

This is a high-risk matter. It combines three separate problems that must be handled on separate tracks: (1) potential safety hazard, (2) a whistleblower/retaliation exposure, (3) a payment demand that may be a legal claim, a settlement negotiation, or something closer to extortion. Treating them as one problem is the most common and most damaging mistake.

I'll give you immediate protective actions first, because some of them are time-sensitive, then the questions I need to go further.

---

## A. Do now (next few hours)

**1. Stop the reply.** No one — especially the CEO — responds to the sender, negotiates, denies, threatens, or acknowledges anything beyond, at most, a neutral "we have received your email and it has been referred for review." Designate a single point of contact (counsel, not the CEO). Every informal reply becomes evidence.

**2. Preservation action should be considered now.** Litigation or an agency proceeding is reasonably anticipated. Suspend routine deletion and issue a litigation hold covering, at minimum:
- **Custodians:** CEO, the former employee's manager(s), HR contacts, plant manager, EHS/safety lead, anyone who handled the separation, anyone named in the safety allegations.
- **Systems:** email, Teams/Slack/text/WhatsApp (including personal devices used for work), the former employee's mailbox and device (do not wipe or re-issue), shared drives, HR/ER files, hotline records, safety incident logs, inspection reports, maintenance and work-order systems, training records, CCTV, badge/access logs, backups.
- **Date span:** from before the alleged conditions began through today, ongoing.
- Log who received the hold, when, how, and acknowledgments.

Do not delete, "clean up," or reorganize anything. Never suggest otherwise to anyone.

**3. Contain circulation.** Route the email and all discussion through counsel, marked *Privileged & Confidential — Attorney-Client Communication / Attorney Work Product — Prepared at the Direction of Counsel*. Need-to-know only. No forwarding to the broader executive team, no group email threads, no commentary about the individual's character or motives.

**4. Assess whether there is a live safety hazard right now.** This is independent of the demand. If the allegations describe an ongoing condition that could injure someone, escalate to EHS/plant leadership immediately for assessment. "We're investigating the messenger" is not a defense if someone gets hurt next week.

**5. Take no adverse action against the sender.** No blacklisting, no negative references, no withholding of already-owed pay or benefits, no contacting their new employer, no public statements. Post-employment retaliation is actionable in many jurisdictions.

**6. Do not call law enforcement or characterize this as "extortion" yet.** It may be. But a criminal referral or an aggressive legal threat against someone raising safety concerns is exactly the fact pattern that converts a manageable dispute into a retaliation claim, an agency complaint, and a press story. That decision needs counsel and a fuller record first.

**7. Flag insurance.** EPLI, D&O, and possibly general liability may require prompt notice of a "claim" or "circumstance." A written demand for money often qualifies. Check policy notice provisions this week.

---

## B. Two tracks, kept separate

**Track 1 — The safety allegations.** These must be investigated on their merits regardless of the sender's motive, the money demand, or whether the person is credible. A company that ignored substantive safety allegations because they arrived with a price tag has no defensible position with a regulator, a jury, or the press. Scope, plan and document a real investigation — ideally directed by counsel for privilege, with EHS and possibly an independent technical reviewer.

**Track 2 — The demand.** Handle as a legal claim/negotiation. Assess what claim (if any) is actually being asserted, whether a release was already signed, what leverage exists, and what resolution is realistic.

Never let Track 2 outcomes drive Track 1 findings, and never document them together.

---

## C. Things that will bite you if you get them wrong

- **You cannot buy silence about safety issues or regulator reporting.** Any settlement must carve out the right to report to and communicate with government agencies (OSHA, and if public, the SEC — Rule 21F-17 gag-clause enforcement is active). A broad confidentiality or non-disparagement clause here can itself be a violation and can void the deal. **Requires current legal verification / jurisdiction-specific review required.**
- **A signed release may not cover everything.** Prior releases typically don't waive the right to file agency charges or protected whistleblower claims.
- **Reporting obligations may already exist** — OSHA recordkeeping/reporting, industry-specific regulators, and, if you are public or have financing covenants, potential disclosure and audit-committee obligations.
- **This may be board-level.** If the allegations implicate senior management, controls, or material safety exposure, the board or audit committee may need to be informed, and management-led investigation may be inappropriate.
- **Whistleblower status doesn't require magic words.** If this person previously raised safety concerns internally, the separation itself is now under scrutiny. Reconstruct that history carefully.

---

## D. What I need from you to go further

1. **Jurisdiction(s)** — plant location(s), and where the former employee worked/resides.
2. **Public or private company?** Any government contracts, union, or regulated industry status?
3. **Exact wording of the email** (paste it, redacted of names if you prefer) — including any stated deadline or threatened recipient (media, OSHA, customers, social media).
4. **Who has seen it, and has anyone responded** in any form, including verbally?
5. **Separation circumstances:** voluntary or involuntary? When? Severance agreement/release signed? Any amounts still unpaid?
6. **Did this person previously raise safety concerns** internally, to the hotline, to a supervisor, or to a regulator? Any documented complaints, near-misses, or incidents at that plant?
7. **Do the allegations describe an ongoing hazard**, and what is your EHS team's initial read?
8. **Is outside counsel already engaged?** Do you have EPLI/D&O coverage?
9. **Who is the decision owner** here, and does the board/audit committee need to be informed under your escalation policy?

Answer these and I'll produce, in order of your priority: a litigation hold notice, a holding response to the sender, an investigation plan and scope memo, and a one-page executive/board briefing.
