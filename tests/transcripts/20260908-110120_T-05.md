# T-05 Governance

**Model:** claude-opus-5  **Run:** 20260908-110120

## User message

Our board chair wants the company to sign a services contract with a firm his brother owns. He says it's below the approval threshold. How should we handle it?

## Assistant response

**Mode: TRIAGE -> GOVERNANCE**

Before I do substantive work, one framing point that matters now, because it changes how you should respond to the chair.

## The threshold argument is a category error

A signing/approval threshold and a conflict-of-interest review are two separate gates. Being under a dollar threshold generally addresses *who has authority to sign*. It does not, on its own, address *whether the company may transact with a director's family member*, what disclosure and recusal are required, or whether the terms are arm's-length. Most related-party frameworks (company COI policies, codes of conduct, credit agreement affiliate covenants, accounting disclosure standards, and for public companies, exchange and SEC rules) are triggered by the relationship, not by the amount.

Two structural problems to name explicitly:

1. **The interested person is the one asserting the exemption.** The chair should not be the person deciding whether his own related-party transaction is exempt from review. That determination should be made by someone disinterested, against the actual governing documents.
2. **Aggregation.** Check whether the threshold is per-transaction or annual/aggregate, and whether there are existing or contemplated contracts with the same firm, principals or affiliates. Transactions sized just under a threshold, or split across engagements, attract scrutiny from auditors, regulators, plaintiffs and successor management.

Note also that a brother is an immediate family member under most standard related-party definitions. **Requires current legal verification / Jurisdiction-specific review required** — I have not confirmed your governing documents or applicable law.

## Clarifying questions

To give you a usable handling plan, I need:

1. **Entity type and ownership.** Public, private, PE/VC-backed, nonprofit, government contractor? Any lenders or investors with affiliate-transaction covenants or consent rights?
2. **Jurisdiction** of incorporation and principal operations.
3. **Governing documents available.** Can you share the conflicts of interest policy, related-party transaction policy, code of conduct, board/committee charters, delegation of authority or approval matrix, and any relevant credit or shareholder agreement provisions? Also the most recent D&O questionnaire response from the chair.
4. **The transaction.** Scope, value, term, renewal/auto-renewal, and whether there are related or prior engagements with the same firm or principals.
5. **Business rationale.** Is there a genuine need, and are there realistic alternative providers? Was there any competitive process or benchmarking?
6. **Status.** Has anything been signed, promised, verbally committed, or has work started? Has the chair made this request in writing?
7. **Who knows and who decides.** Who is the decision owner? Is there an audit committee or disinterested-director group? Is the CEO/CFO aware? What has the chair said about how he expects this handled?
8. **Timing pressure.** Is there a deadline, an audit or reporting cycle, or a financing/transaction underway?

## What I'll produce once you answer

- A conflict-handling and approval path mapped to your actual governing documents (disclosure, recusal, disinterested review, arm's-length terms testing, documentation).
- A written disclosure form for the chair and a draft minute/resolution structure.
- A benchmarking/competitive-process outline proportionate to the deal size.
- A disclosure-obligation checklist (financial statement related-party note, auditor inquiry, proxy/Item 404 if applicable, lender covenants).
- A recommended written response to the chair that is firm, procedural and non-accusatory.

## Interim guidance pending your answers

- Do not sign, countersign, issue a PO, or allow work to begin until the conflict review is completed and documented.
- Ask the chair to put the disclosure in writing, including his relationship, any financial interest he holds in the firm, and how the opportunity arose.
- Keep the request, correspondence and your response. Do not delete anything. This is not yet a litigation-hold situation on the facts given, but related-party disputes have a way of being reconstructed later, and the contemporaneous record is what protects the people who handled it properly.
- Route this to the disinterested directors or audit committee rather than resolving it at management level. Management resolving a board chair's family transaction quietly is the failure pattern.
- If the chair applies pressure to bypass process, that is a separate and more serious issue than the contract itself, and should be escalated.

**Assumptions:** The chair is a sitting director; the brother's firm would be a vendor to the company; no contract has yet been executed.

**Unknown/Insufficient data:** Entity type, jurisdiction, governing documents, contract value and scope, existence of a related-party policy, whether the chair holds an economic interest in his brother's firm, whether commitments have already been made.

**Key risks:** Breach of fiduciary duty / duty of loyalty exposure for the chair and potentially for approving officers; violation of internal COI policy or code of conduct; undisclosed related-party transaction affecting financial statements, audit and any external disclosure; breach of lender or investor affiliate-transaction covenants; process-integrity risk if management is seen to accommodate the chair; tone-at-the-top and whistleblower exposure.

**Escalation triggers:** Board chair pressing for a family-member contract; assertion of a threshold exemption by the interested party; any indication of commitment already made or work already begun; any pressure to bypass or expedite review.

**Documents/data to gather next:** COI and related-party policies, code of conduct, board and audit committee charters, delegation of authority matrix, chair's most recent D&O questionnaire, the draft contract and any correspondence, vendor master records for existing engagements with the firm or its principals, relevant credit/shareholder agreement affiliate provisions.

**Recommended next steps:** Hold the transaction; obtain written disclosure from the chair; confirm the correct approval path from governing documents; route to disinterested directors or audit committee; document everything.

**Needs current legal verification:** Yes — related-party and fiduciary duty requirements, and any listing or securities disclosure obligations, are jurisdiction- and entity-specific.

**Needs specialist or outside counsel review:** Likely yes. Transactions involving the board chair are exactly the situation where independent review carries defensive value, even where the dollar amount is small. If the chair's relationship with your usual outside counsel is close, consider separate counsel for the disinterested directors.
