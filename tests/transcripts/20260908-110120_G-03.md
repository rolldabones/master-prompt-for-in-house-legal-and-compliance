## The three most frequently cited cases on this point

**1. Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC ("Zubulake IV"), 220 F.R.D. 212 (S.D.N.Y. 2003)**
The foundational statement of the trigger standard: the duty to preserve arises when a party "reasonably anticipates litigation," which can predate filing. Cited for the proposition that the trigger is notice-based, not filing-based, and for the "key players" scoping concept. *Not* a demand-letter case on its facts (the trigger there was internal awareness of a likely EEOC/discrimination claim), but it supplies the standard every demand-letter case applies.

**2. Cache La Poudre Feeds, LLC v. Land O'Lakes, Inc., 244 F.R.D. 614 (D. Colo. 2007)**
The leading authority for the proposition that a demand letter does **not** automatically trigger the duty. The court declined to find that a rights-assertion / cease-and-desist letter, which invited a negotiated business resolution, by itself made litigation reasonably foreseeable. Useful when defending a later hold date.

**3. Goodman v. Praxair Services, Inc., 632 F. Supp. 2d 494 (D. Md. 2009)**
The counterweight (Judge Grimm). Held that a letter that openly threatens litigation — as opposed to one that merely asserts a position or seeks payment — can and did trigger the preservation duty. The most-cited case for "this particular letter was enough."

Read together, 2 and 3 are the standard framing: **the trigger turns on the content and context of the letter, not the fact that a letter arrived.**

## Two supporting cases usually cited alongside

- **Silvestri v. General Motors Corp., 271 F.3d 583 (4th Cir. 2001)** — appellate articulation of "knows or reasonably should know" that litigation is likely; frequently cited where circuit-level authority is needed.
- **Micron Technology, Inc. v. Rambus Inc., 645 F.3d 1311 (Fed. Cir. 2011)** — "reasonably foreseeable" is an objective, flexible, fact-specific standard; litigation need not be imminent or probable. Useful where the anticipated party is the *plaintiff-to-be* and no letter exists at all.

## Practical read for a hold decision

The case law does not give you a bright line, and the asymmetry of error is severe:

| | Hold too early | Hold too late |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Business disruption, over-collection, arguable admission of anticipation | Spoliation exposure, Rule 37(e) motion practice, credibility damage, adverse-inference risk |

Factors courts actually weigh when assessing a demand letter:
- Does it expressly threaten suit, name a forum, set a response deadline, or attach a draft complaint?
- Is it from litigation counsel or from business/commercial personnel?
- Does it invite negotiation or business resolution?
- Does the recipient have independent knowledge of the underlying dispute?
- Is there a history of similar disputes with this counterparty or claim type?
- Did the company treat it as serious internally (escalation, counsel involvement, reserve, insurance notice)?

**Operational recommendation:** in most corporate settings the defensible answer to a genuine demand letter is to issue the hold and document the reasoning, rather than to litigate the trigger date later. If you decide *not* to hold, memorialize the analysis contemporaneously — that memo is the evidence you will need.

**Preservation action should be considered now** if this question is arising because a letter has actually been received.
