## User message

Our hotline received a report that a VP of Sales has been approving fake expense reports for a vendor owned by his cousin. Help me get started.

## Assistant response

**Mode: TRIAGE → INVESTIGATION SUPPORT → ESCALATION**

## Initial read

This is a **high-risk matter** and triggers escalation even before more facts are known:

- **Alleged fraud and possible embezzlement.** Fictitious expenses mean company funds may have gone to a party with no legitimate entitlement.
- **Senior personnel.** A VP is the alleged approver, so ordinary management-line reporting is likely compromised.
- **Undisclosed related-party or conflict-of-interest dimension.** The vendor is reportedly owned by the VP's cousin.
- **Potential internal-controls failure.** One person both had the conflict and held approval authority.
- **Possible financial-reporting, disclosure, tax or insurance consequences.** These depend on your company type, amounts and policies.

The report is an **allegation**, not a finding. Use "alleged" and "reported" in all writing, and avoid "fake," "fraud" or "kickback" as conclusions.

## Points to consider now

These don't depend on further answers.

1. **Route through Legal.** Have the matter directed by in-house counsel, or outside counsel if retained, to support privilege. Mark communications "Privileged & Confidential, Attorney-Client Communication / Prepared at Direction of Counsel" where accurate. Note that labels alone don't create privilege.
2. **Restrict circulation to need-to-know.** Do not inform the VP, his manager or his team. Be cautious with Sales Ops and any AP staff who work closely with him.
3. **Preservation action should be considered now.** There is a tension here: a standard custodian hold notice to the VP could tip him off. Consider **quiet IT-side preservation first**, coordinated through Legal and a trusted IT contact:
   - The VP's mailbox, messaging, calendar and OneDrive or equivalent, with auto-deletion suspended
   - Expense-system and AP records, including approval logs and audit trails
   - Vendor master-file records: onboarding, bank details, W-9 or equivalent, who added the vendor
   - Company devices assigned to the VP

   Document what was preserved, by whom, when and how.
4. **Protect the reporter.** Assume retaliation risk. Limit any information that could identify the reporter. Do not attempt to unmask an anonymous reporter.
5. **Consider insurance notice.** Crime, fidelity or employee-dishonesty policies often have **discovery-based notice deadlines**. Ask risk management or the broker to review the policy terms promptly.
6. **Decide on vendor payments, but don't act alone.** Freezing payments to the vendor may protect funds but could tip off the subject. Treat this as a decision for the matter owner. It is not an automatic step.
7. **Don't interview the VP yet.** Subject interviews generally come after document review.

## Escalation flag

- **Why:** Credible-on-its-face fraud allegation against senior personnel, involving a conflict of interest and a control failure.
- **To whom:** General Counsel or CLO and the Chief Compliance Officer. Likely also the **Audit Committee chair**, depending on your whistleblower policy and charter. This is especially likely if you are public or SOX-subject, or if the amount could be material. Consider the CFO only if the CFO is not in the VP's approval chain or otherwise conflicted.
- **Urgency:** Within days, not weeks. Do it immediately if the alleged activity is ongoing.
- **Gather first:** The verbatim hotline report, the vendor's payment history, and the VP's approval history for that vendor.
